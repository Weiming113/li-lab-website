Research

How extreme physiological stress reshapes cell state, transcription, and epigenomes — and how those programs drive inflammation and organ dysfunction.

Research Overview

Our lab seeks to understand how extreme physiological stresses — cardiopulmonary bypass and cardiac arrest — reshape cell state, cause changes in transcription and epigenomics, and ultimately drive inflammation and organ dysfunction. We integrate single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, epigenomics, computational biology, and mechanistic experimentation with clinically relevant models and human samples to uncover the molecular programs that connect physiological stress to inflammatory response and disease. The ultimate goal is to improve the outcomes for patients with cardiovascular disease, with a particular focus on improving survival and long-term neurological and functional outcomes in the neonates and children undergoing critical cardiovascular care.

Projects in the Lab

Poor neurological outcomes following cardiac arrest (CA)

Cardiac arrest (CA) is a life-threatening condition that can affect infants and adults. In the United States, more than 15,000 pediatric CA patients occur annually, with mortality rates of 80–90% prior to hospital discharge. Post-CA syndrome encompasses a complex set of pathophysiological processes, including brain injury in variable regions, myocardial dysfunction and persistent pathophysiology. Neurological outcomes following CA are a primary focus of clinical care, as neurological injury is a major determinant of both survival and long-term functional recovery. The initial priority of post-CA is to identify and reverse the underlying cause of CA in clinical practice. Subsequently, strategies such as therapeutic hypothermia, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) have shown to improve neurological outcomes. In addition, high-dose valproic acid (VPA) in combination with hypothermia has been reported to synergistically improve neurologic outcomes after CA. Nevertheless, despite advances in post-CA care, the overall outcomes of post-CA patients remain poor. Progress in developing effective therapies has been hindered by a limited mechanistic understanding of post-CA injury. This knowledge gap is largely attributable to the limitations of existing laboratory models, the heterogeneity of patient populations, and variations in post-resuscitation care. My preliminary studies were among the first to use single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches to define cell type-specific gene expression changes in the cerebral cortex of both humans and piglets after CA, as well as after ECMO and VPA treatment. By combining a clinically relevant piglet model with human brain tissue analyses, my research will bridge the gap between experimental models and human disease, providing mechanistic insights into CA-associated brain injury.

Project objectives

  1. Determine cell-type-specific vulnerability in the cerebral cortex following CA.
  2. Identify conserved transcriptional regulatory networks and stress-response programs underlying post-CA brain injury.
  3. Leverage conserved regulatory networks to identify mechanism-based therapeutic targets and treatment-response programs following CA.

Clinical issues caused by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)

Most cardiac surgery patients require CPB to limit ischemic damage while providing surgeons with a bloodless operative field. CPB involves exposing the blood of patients to several non-physiological stimuli that include mechanical shear stress, direct blood contact with foreign surface, and reduced body temperature. The significantly systemic post-CPB inflammation is one of the main causes of CPB-related morbidity. The significant post-CPB inflammation manifests in several ways: increased cytokines, inflammatory cell infiltration and vascular leak, and multi-organ dysfunctions. Pediatric patients are more prone to post-CPB inflammation, and the consequences can be more severe, such as a higher risk for neurodevelopmental issues and renal dysfunction. My previous study identified a shear stress-responsive SPTAN1/RAF1/store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) signaling pathway that drives inflammatory responses, including IL-8 production, in non-adherent immune cells (THP-1). Pharmacological targeting of this pathway alleviated CPB-induced inflammation, revealing a key mechanism underlying shear stress-induced sterile inflammation. Despite these advances, whether and how CPB affects patient outcomes by altering immune cell differentiation and driving persistent pathophysiological changes remains unknown.

Project objectives

  1. Determine how CPB reshapes monocyte, T-cell, and B-cell differentiation programs and immune-cell states.
  2. Identify regulatory elements and transcriptional regulators that drive CPB-induced monocyte reprogramming and inflammation.
  3. Determine mechanisms of candidate drug response in CPB-associated inflammation.
